Chapter 6: PLANT POPULATION DYNAMICS

Studies of animal populations involving a census of their numbers have occupied a central position in animal ecology for a considerable period of time. Plant ecology however, has been largely dominated by investigations into the spatial configurations of species in relation to their environment. At a small scale such configurations are termed pattern (Chapter 7) and at larger scales, plant associations, which can be characterized by their 'unique' species spectrum (Chapters 9 and 10). Until relatively recently very little attention has been focused on plant populations and the control of the numbers of individuals involved. This lack of information partly stems from the pre-occupation with spatial configurations and partly from the difficulties inherent in studying changes in natural populations of plants. In animal populations changes can be fairly readily documented by changes in numbers. In plant populations, changes in numbers of individuals do occur, but more frequently a population change is initially reflected by much less obvious criteria. Changes in dry weight, growth morphology, number of seeds per individual, all indicate pressure on the population from environmental changes or by increased interference from other species. A change in actual numbers of mature individuals is a much more gradual process, especially where perennial species are involved. Understandably what information is available is largely derived from experimental studies on populations of annual weed species, which with their short life cycle are more amenable to an experimental approach. The evidence available for perennial species suggests that at least the numbers of the population established by seed are controlled in the same way as annual species.

Harper (1977) has extensively reviewed in depth the development of plant population dynamics whilst Silvertown (1982) has provided an excellent yet more concise coverage of the topic. Only examples of the more salient points are covered here and these are summarized as a simple flow diagram for two hypothetical species A and B (Fig. 6.1). Each stage of the life cycle which interacts with or is affected by either an environmental parameter or another species is shown. Equally the overall importance of season, biotic factors, and genetical make-up of the potential population is indicated.

Events in the soil

The number of individuals reaching maturity in a population at any given point in time is clearly not only a function of how many seeds are available, but also of what physiological state they are in. Many plant species produce seeds which do not germinate immediately but which lie dormant until a particular sequence of either intrinsic events, extrinsic events, or both, trigger germination. Harper (1977) conveniently classifies dormancy into three categories: innate dormancy, which is a specific property of a species and requires a particular treatment to break it; induced dormancy which can develop when a non-dormant seed is exposed to a particular (harsh) environmental situation; and enforced dormancy developed in response to external conditions but removed immediately when the conditions are suitably modified.

Increasing O, tension around the seed embryo by pricking or abrading the seed coat, giving a fluctuating temperature or low temperature treatment, light treatment, or an increased level of nitrate availability, have all been shown to induce germination (Stokes, 1965; Thurston, 1959; Toole et al., 1956; Vegis, 1963). All of these parameters are likely to be operative in all plant systems. Seed coats decay with time allowing an increased oxygen supply to the embryo and equally temperature fluctuates seasonally. Since nitrate concentration also varies throughout the season due to the temperature dependence of nitrogen-fixing organisms, the control of the actual number of seeds available for germination at time N is extremely complex.

Popay and Roberts (1970a and b) examined the emergence of populations of the weed species Senecio vulgaris and Capsella bursa-pastoris over a two-year period and correlated in part the observed flushes of germination with the breaking of dormancy.

Growth and interaction

Black (1955, 1956, 1958, 1960) in an elegant series of papers examined the population numbers of subterranean clover, and how they changed. They are especially interesting since they demonstrate one of the mechanisms of interference pressure between individuals.

The influence of seed size on the area of the cotyledons has been discussed briefly before (p. 99, Fig. 6.7) and similarly the interaction with depth of sowing. Black (1955, 1956) showed that seed size determined the depth from which successful seedling emergence can take place and also the initial area of cotyledons. It appears that once successful emergence has been achieved the area of the cotyledons determines the extent of the difference in the early stages of growth. It should be noted that this relationship only holds for seeds with epigeal germination and where the whole seed is embryo plus coat and there is no endosperm.

Thus seed size is merely one factor in a complex system which interacts with one or several other factors to decide the final outcome of population balance. Black's data demonstrate a situation where the initial slight advantage of one seedling over another becomes exaggerated by other factors in the system. Conversely, Harper's data show a system where the reverse is true and potential deleterious effects of seed size are eliminated by other factors in the system, again emphasizing the extremely complex control mechanisms of population numbers.

It should be recognized that the interaction of the environment", which may also include intrinsic properties of each species in addition to climate, occurs not only during establishment and early growth, but is a continuous process that also operates on mature annuals or perennials. This level of simplification is also usually inherent in the field studies so far completed.

Depletion and survivorship curves in natural populations

A population of individuals is in a state of continual flux as the unit constituents die, survive or reproduce.

There has been some discussion as to the exact form a unit

constituent of the population takes. Thus it has been argued that the number of embryos within a population of seeds, for example, that reaches maturity constitutes the primary population in any one year. A proportion of these may be destroyed by pathogens, grazed by herbivores, or over-stressed by macroclimate.

Subsequently there is potentially a further sizeable decline of the seedling population in the soil, before even any seedlings can be identified and any further population changes documented. However, because of the formidable, if not impossible, nature of following such pre-germination events with accuracy, it has been standard practice to accept the population unit as an emergent seedling, where establishment by seed is important, or where a vegetative unit is involved, some measure of the stolon/rhizome number provides the established population. These two forms of recruits' are termed genets (Kays and Harper, 1974; Harper, 1977) and ramets respectively. A genet is a single seedling or, in many perennial plants, an extensive clone of individual rhizomes which then can be subdivided into ramet units. Normally genets are expressed by N whereas n is reserved for the number of ramets per genet, and thus Nn gives the total number of propagules (Harper, 1977) present in the whole population.

There are several ways of expressing the changes in numbers of individuals of a species with time. In Fig. 6.19 the changes in number of individuals of Ranunculus acris (Sarukhán and Harper, 1973) are expressed over a two year experimental period in different ways. Thus there is a 'net' population at any point in time which cycles regularly up and down as individuals are recruited from seed or as seedlings and mature individuals are eliminated from the population. However, this net population can also be examined as a sequence of new recruits added to the existing population or as a sequence of deaths and this information is best presented as cumulative gains and losses. Two points then clearly emerge: (1) although there is little difference between the absolute numbers of individuals at the end of each of the two years there has been a tremendous flux of individuals over the experimental period; (2) there is a very marked period of synchronous recruitment and mortality, with an almost constant level of survivorship throughout the intervening periods of the year. Examination of net numbers together with cumulative gains and losses does not however give any information of events which may correlate with specific age groups of individuals. At the start of an experimental period the age structure of the population is clearly unknown. However, if each individual is tagged, subsequent annual recruitment to the population can then be documented together with the subsequent mortality. The term cohort has been applied extensively to the group of individuals that are recruited in any one year, i.e. one generation of recruits.

Detailed analysis of each group of cohorts can then provide an accurate survivorship curve on an annual basis. Such curves are age-specific (see for example Angevine,

1983\) and contrast with depletion curves which follow the numbers of plants of all ages that survive.

Established individuals over winter as vegetative rosettes with an almost constant but low mortality rate until late March when flowering and anthesis occurs. During this period greater demands are made on the energy resources of the environment as a result of this growth and development but at the same time the seedlings also experience interference both from each other as well as from other associated species. This results in a slight increase in the rate of mortality. Harper also provides survivorship curves for other annual species (Fig. 6.21), and it is evident that increased levels of mortality are often (although not always) associated with specific seasonal events, particularly during the initial establishment period and/or during reproductive phases. Similarly, Ranunculus acris (Fig. 6.19) clearly has a rate of mortality more or less restricted to the establishment phase.

Perennial species also show a higher risk of mortality associated with seedling establishment as well as with the season of most active growth. However, if the reproductive unit is vegetative, usually the mortality risk associated with the establishment phase is little different from that experienced by fully established mature plants. However, over several years, established ramets and genets typically show a log-linear decline in numbers of survivors with a slight superimposed seasonal pattern of increased and decreased mortality. Thus it appears that a more or less constant interference pressure controls survivorship of cohorts over time, rather than year-to-year variations of macro- or microclimate. Despite the prolific work that has appeared on plant demography over the last decade, part of which has been well summarised by Harper (1977), depressingly few general concepts have emerged. Equally unfortunately a good proportion of these data are relentlessly descriptive with very little attempt to relate the findings to potential mechanisms of change. Genetic control, seed production and total system interaction

Overall control of the growth pattern and behaviour is controlled by the genetic make-up of the individuals, and in many instances occurs at a level where phenotypic differences are not necessarily evident. Black (1960) showed that three strains of subterranean clover with leaf development at different heights above ground (Fig. 6.23) produced different light profiles resulting in marked interference between individuals in mixed populations (Fig. 6.24). Cavers and Harper (1966) using replicates of individuals of Rumex crispus and R. obtusifolius from six different locations showed an extremely variable response to two contrasting germination conditions (Fig. 6.25).

Not only were there marked differences between locations but different individuals from the same site showed as much variation (Fig. 6.26). Although this polymorphism can be related to the nanoclimate of different parts of the inflorescence, or the microclimate of different parts of the site, it is highly probable that there remains a residuum of variability directly related to genetic control. These two cases suggest for some species at least, that predictions as to population behaviour pattern will have to allow for the distribution of the gene population and its effect on seed germination and seedling development. There is now available a wide range of well-documented examples of ecotypic variation which has been very usefully collated by Heslop-Harrison (1964). In addition to relatively gross morphological differences (leaf lengths, hairiness, etc.) there is a wide range of physiological ecotypes. McKell et al. (1960) compared two races of Dactylis glomerata and showed very different responses to differing conditions of soil moisture stress, which correlated with their known distribution. Björkman and Holmgren (1963) in a study of photosynthetic efficiency in the sun and shade ecotypes of Solidago virgaurea found a more efficient use of weak light in shade ecotypes than in those from open habitats.

Conversely,

clones from open habitats utilised intense light more efficiently.

Similarly, Mooney and Billings (1961) for Oxyria digyna showed that plants of northern populations had higher respiration rates at all temperatures than plants of southern alpine populations. Numerous additional examples are also available in the literature. Although many of these ecotypic differences relate to geographically separated populations, similar though less extreme variability will occur in adjacent populations, where environmental pressure has been exerted on the gene population.

Equally, although the documented examples above do not relate directly to seed characteristics, they are all indirectly related and will control to a greater or lesser extent seed numbers, or quantity of stored metabolites and hence seed size.

Thus the level of complexity in even a ' simple' system is formidable and this has prompted a number of ecologists to provide a rather bleak assessment of current and future progress (Grime, 1984; Harper, 1982; Woolhouse, 1981). This has prompted Grime (1979; 1984) and others to examine alternatives to the rather descriptive approaches that have very much dominated community and population ecology.

Thus information on the contrasting field distributions of a range of plants and animals reveals the importance of sets of traits which in turn has focussed attention on particular strategies, the most widely known being that of r- and K- selection (MacArthur and Wilson, 1967; Pianka, 1983). The term strategy, when used in an ecological context, has attracted some criticism, but in agreement with Grime

(1984), we retain the concept here as a grouping of similar or analogous genetic characteristics that recur repeatedly among species or populations and underlie the similarities of their ecology.

Despite the apparent ready and embracing acceptance of r-and K-selection, it is quite incomplete in terms of the full range of plant ecology (see Grime, 1979 particularly), and the alternative triangular-model approach proposed by Grime

(1974; 1979; 1984) offers a more satisfactory alternative. The model involves three primary strategies characteristic of competitors (C), ruderal species (R) and those species that tolerate stress (S), respectively (Fig. 6.29). This approach provides a framework in which information from a range of ecological studies can be related together.
